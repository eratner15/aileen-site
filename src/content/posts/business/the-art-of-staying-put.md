I've driven 1,600 miles across America this summer, chasing a feeling I couldn't name. What I found was stranger than I expected. The places that feel most American are the ones that shouldn't work at all.

The coffee shop in Boulder where I'm standing now looks identical to one I visited in Nashville three months ago. 

Same exposed brick. Same Edison bulbs. Same mural celebrating "local artists."

An America of algorithmic aesthetics, where authentic artisanal atmosphere arrives as an *available, adaptable* template.

Four hundred miles north, half a million bikers converge on a town so remote that South Dakota needs temporary work visas to staff the seasonal surge. The bikers sleep in tents, eat questionable food, and create something no urban planner managed to manufacture: *actual* community.

Between these extremes lies the real story of American belonging. Not in the sameness, but in the stubborn refusal to *be* the same. In steam rooms where conversation happens without agenda. In mountain towns where thin air forces you to slow down or pass out.

This is a story of American friction, stillness, and the radical act of choosing where you belong.

## THE GREAT AMERICAN COPY-PASTE

In every American city with between 200,000 and two million residents, the transformation follows the same script. 

The formula is foolproof: mixed-use development, street-level retail, luxury apartments above. Just add a Whole Foods and watch the magic happen.

Walk through Nashville's *Gulch*, Tampa's *Westshore*, or Boulder's *Pearl Street*, and you'll encounter identical results: glass condos named *The Independent*, *Elevé 61*, or *The Modern*. Street-level retail featuring **Blue Bottle** coffee, **SoulCycle** fitness, **Sweetgreen** bowls, wine bars with "*curated small plates*" and mysterious "*lifestyle activation spaces*" that are co-working spaces with better marketing.

**Cities became brands. Brands became algorithms. Algorithms became indistinguishable.**

The economic logic is airtight - *why risk innovation when you can replicate success? *

As Richard Florida documented in his 2002 book 'The Rise of the Creative Class,' cities compete for the same college-educated professionals who drive economic growth. That winning formula has been reverse-engineered and mass-produced.

According to the Urban Land Institute, 73 percent of mixed-use developments in mid-sized American cities follow the template, up from 31 percent in 2000.

*We're building the same city over and over again, changing only the ZIP code.*

### WHEN THE ALGORITHM FAILS

Sometimes the template transcends itself.

Austin's South Congress should be another algorithmic neighborhood. The boutique hotels and farm-to-table restaurants hit every expected note. But something survived: the costume shop from 1980, music venues predating gentrification, food trailers that became institutions instead of Instagram opportunities.

![](https://substack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com/public/images/83eac6ab-fafb-4668-ae0a-337f1b582d54_1024x1024.png)

South Congress maintains what urbanist** Jane Jacob**s called "*organized complexity,*" messy, unplanned interactions that make cities feel alive not merely functional.

Maybe the commitment to *Keep Austin Weird* means that no algorithm could flatten it. But for every South Congress, there are dozens of neighborhoods ordered from a catalog called *Urban Authenticity: Some Assembly Required*.

## WHERE AMERICA GOES TO REMEMBER ITSELF

Drive into Sturgis, South Dakota, and reality hits like a V-twin backfire at 6 a.m.

This state is so empty that it needs only one area code. Yet each year, Main Street becomes a river of chrome and leather. Motorcycles park nose-to-tail for blocks, engines ticking like metallic insects cooling in the sun. Sidewalks pulse with riders in worn denim and tour shirts from rallies past, moving between tattoo parlors, dive bars, and vendors selling everything from custom exhaust pipes to *If You Can Read This, The Bitch Fell Off* t-shirts.

Every August since 1938, this prairie town of 7,000 has become America's temporary third-largest city. Peak attendance hit 739,000 in 2015, more than Wyoming’s population, compressed into a handful of streets never designed for this beautiful chaos.

The air vibrates with sound and scent: Harley rumbles, bouncing off storefront glass, the pneumatic hiss of beer cans opening, grilled onions mixing with gasoline and sun-baked leather. At campsites like Buffalo Chip, riders pitch tents between their bikes like protective parents, cooking bacon on manifolds while trading stories of breakdowns overcome and cops evaded.

![](https://substack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com/public/images/2798312f-67a9-42b0-b90f-38ba88a6b3c9_1456x816.png)

Stand still for thirty seconds on Main Street during the rally, and something shifts. The chaos reorganizes itself into ritual. The randomness reveals its patterns. Everyone present in their discomfort, present in their choice to be here. This isn't disorder, it's connection with the volume turned up.

What makes Sturgis extraordinary isn't the spectacle, it's the pilgrimage. The Sturgis Motorcycle Museum reports that 60 percent are repeat visitors, many claiming the same camping spots. Not because it's convenient or comfortable, but because they've found something worth the 2,000-mile ride: a place that belongs to them as much as they belong to it.

Bikers converge from everywhere. Quebec plates lean against Alabama tags. A group from Oregon camps beside Florida retirees. The South Dakota Department of Tourism reports the rally generates over $800 million in statewide economic activity, with $130 million flowing directly into the four-county Black Hills region. That's nearly 15 percent of the area's annual tourism revenue compressed into ten days.

## THE BEAUTIFUL CONTRADICTION

The Americans who best understand staying put are the most relentlessly mobile. 

These riders burn more gasoline in ten days than most use in a year, to pitch tents in the same patch of dirt they've claimed for decades. They've turned migration into a form of marriage, not fleeing from place to place like digital nomads hunting better WiFi, but weaponizing radical freedom of movement to choose the same imperfect home, over and over again.

![](https://substack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com/public/images/c25a4b4d-27eb-47c8-b89e-128090128c3d_1024x1024.png)
Do you think they know we're from Connecticut?
While lifestyle migrants optimize from Austin to Boulder to Portland searching for the perfect intersection of kombucha availability and property values, bikers ride 2,000 miles to return to mediocre amenities. 

That's not mobility. That's *devotion* with a Harley engine.

This devotion to difficult places extends beyond Sturgis, carved into the mountains that frame it. Fifty-seven miles apart, two monuments tell competing stories about American ambition, commitment, and the meaning of completion itself.

## WHEN COMPLETION IS THE ENEMY 

**Mount Rushmore**, completed in 1941 by sculptor and Stamford, CT native **Gutzon Borglum**, stands as the ultimate optimized tourist experience. The four presidential faces lit perfectly for photographs, surrounded by visitor facilities designed for maximum throughput. 

Entry costs $25 per vehicle for a year-long parking pass, though most visitors spend 2.3 hours on average, according to the National Park Service. You can experience the entirety of this national monument, capture your proof-of-visit selfie, purchase commemorative items, and return to your car in less time than a movie.

![](https://substack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com/public/images/88843d4b-c834-4df9-8eb2-606abac7888b_1680x720.png)

Seventeen miles away, **Crazy Horse Memorial** tells a radically different story. 

Sculptor **Korczak Ziolkowski** began carving the 563-foot-tall mountain sculpture in 1948, knowing he would never see its completion. When he died in 1982, his children inherited both mountain and commitment. Today, his sons and daughters continue with hand tools and dynamite, funded entirely by private donations and $15 per person admission fees.

The memorial's completion timeline remains deliberately vague. "*When is it finished?*" reads their FAQ. "*The Memorial is a work in progress, and will remain so for many years to come."*

Mount Rushmore is a completed transaction, a finished product designed for efficient public consumption. 

Crazy Horse embodies something almost extinct in American culture: multi-generational commitment to a project that transcends individual lifetimes.

Standing between these monuments, you're witnessing two competing philosophies of place and purpose. One celebrates what's finished. The other celebrates work itself.

## PARTY OF ONE

Statistics paint a stark picture of American rootlessness. The U.S. Census Bureau shows the average American moves nearly a dozen times in their life, a restlessness that's increased 30 percent since 1970. 

Meanwhile, membership in civic organizations has collapsed catastrophically. Research from the National Conference on Citizenship shows participation in community organizations dropped 65 percent between 1975 and 2020. 

![](https://substack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com/public/images/22555aac-21f1-4cb3-8c2a-b3b6dcc69964_1024x584.png)

As** Robert Putnam**, political scientist, put it, we're now all *"bowling alone"*. Two-thirds of Americans can't name five people on their own street.

Yet developers market 'community-focused' neighborhoods promising connection without sacrifice, identity without commitment, belonging without investment. The template offers the aesthetic of community while requiring none of the sustained presence that actually creates it.

## THE WISDOM OF INCONVENIENCE

Sturgis succeeds because it refuses optimization. Accommodations are overpriced, food ranges from acceptable to questionable, and noise levels illegal anywhere else. The entire experience is architected around inconvenience, discomfort, and chaos that sends urban planners into fetal positions.

This isn't a bug. It's the central feature.

When everything is convenient, nothing binds people. When comfort is guaranteed, you never need neighbors. When experiences are frictionless, nothing requires the kind of mutual aid and collaborative problem-solving that builds lasting relationships.

Friction isn't incidental to the community. Friction is the community.

But friction isn't the only path to community. 

Drive a few hours south in Colorado Springs, a different truth emerges: sometimes all you need is a gym and a shvitz.

### THE OTHER PATH

In the Colorado Country Club's steam room, conversation happens without agenda. Members from different generations, professions, and backgrounds sit in shared silence or trade stories while bodies surrender to the heat. This involuntary deceleration creates space for a connection that doesn't exist in the optimized world outside

No phones. No hurry. Just *presence*.

![](https://substack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com/public/images/64ad8529-134d-4d7e-af47-2a34be946db6_1024x1024.png)

Vail and Breckenridge understand this intuitively. Locals move at mountain time, a high-altitude cousin of island time. Chairlift conversations, three-hour lunches, acceptance that thin air won't let you rush. Living above 8,000 feet changes your rhythm. The landscape demands stillness.

Two paths, same destination. Bikers found that friction creates connection, difficulty demands commitment. Mountain towns discovered the opposite: community emerges from collective slowdown, from shared permission to step outside optimization's relentless anxiety.

![](https://substack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com/public/images/d0942d8e-2698-4eb0-8444-7aba53def47e_1456x816.png)

Both interrupt America's efficiency addiction. Whether through hardship or ease, they create spaces where relationships form without purpose, where presence becomes more valuable than productivity.

The question isn't which path to choose. It's whether you'll choose at all.

## YOUR CRAZY HORSE, YOUR STEAM ROOM

Standing in yet another Edison-bulb-lit coffee shop in yet another "*vibrant mixed-use development*," scrolling through real estate listings seeking the perfect intersection of walkability scores and property values, we’re asking the wrong questions.

Instead of "*Where can I find community*?" try: "What community will I build?"

Also ask: "Where can I be still?"

Whether it's the chaos of a biker rally or the pause of a steam room, these places resist the efficiency that enslaves us. 

We've optimized ourselves into constant tension, chasing productivity even during rest. Mountain towns know something we’ve forgotten: sometimes the most radical act is doing *nothing at all*.  

![](https://substack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com/public/images/e5d844a3-64db-4cb5-a6bc-66575a3a7610_1456x816.png)

**What's your Crazy Horse? **

The impossible project worth starting, though your great-grandchildren might finish it. The place worth committing to when it's not optimized for your satisfaction. The people worth showing up for when it's inconvenient.

**What's your steam room? **

The space where you shed the anxiety of constant productivity and remember connection often emerges in stillness, not motion.

Bikers and mountain dwellers understand what template cities miss. Optimization is the enemy of both commitment and presence. Presence, whether forged through shared difficulty or shared ease, is the foundation of everything real.

Real community isn't something you discover doomscrolling or purchase through a lease agreement. It's something you construct, deliberately and patiently, one year at a time. Or one quiet moment at a time.

Everything else is just *tourism.*

***The rarest American virtue isn't authenticity. It’s presence.***
