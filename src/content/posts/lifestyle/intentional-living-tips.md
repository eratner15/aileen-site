I didn't arrive at intentional living through some elegant revelation. I arrived at it because I was tired. Tired of the low-grade hum of a day that felt full but never felt finished. Tired of opening a closet packed with clothes and finding nothing to wear. Tired of reaching for my phone forty times before noon and not remembering why.

If you're looking for intentional living tips that go beyond "buy less stuff," you're in the right place. Because this isn't about deprivation. It's about deciding — clearly, on purpose — what gets your time, your attention, and your square footage. The kind of shift that doesn't photograph well but changes everything underneath.

## Why Most Intentional Living Tips Miss the Point

There's a particular brand of intentional living content that treats the whole thing like a Pinterest board. White walls. Empty counters. A single orchid. And sure, a clear surface is lovely. But if you've cleared the counter and still feel scattered at 2 p.m., the counter was never the problem.

Real intentionality starts with awareness. Not aesthetics.

It means noticing the patterns you've been running on autopilot — the way you scroll before your feet hit the floor, the way you say yes to things that drain you, the way you accumulate objects not because you love them but because returning them felt like too much effort.

The women I admire most aren't the ones with the least. They're the ones who seem to have chosen everything in their lives on purpose. Their homes feel considered. Their schedules have breathing room. They're not rushing, and they're not performing calm either. They just look like they know what matters to them.

That's the goal. Not less. Just chosen.

## Start With the Morning — It Sets the Temperature for Everything

I've written about this before in my piece on building a [morning routine that feels elegant rather than punishing](/magazine/lifestyle/morning-routine-elegant/), and I stand by every word. How you spend the first hour of your day is the most honest measure of your priorities.

For a long time, my mornings were reactive. Eyes open, phone on, inbox first. By the time I'd made coffee I'd already absorbed someone else's urgency. The day felt borrowed from the start.

Now the phone stays in the kitchen until after breakfast. I drink my coffee hot — actually hot, not reheated three times. I write three lines in a notebook. Not journaling in any formal sense. Just three lines about what I want the day to feel like.

It takes twelve minutes. Maybe fifteen. But those minutes are mine, and they set a tone that carries.

Here's what I'd suggest if mornings feel chaotic: don't add anything. Subtract. Remove the thing that's hijacking your attention before you've had a chance to decide where it goes. For most of us, that thing has a screen.

## Your Home Should Work for the Life You Actually Live

I spent years curating a home that looked right in photos. Styled bookshelves. Throw pillows in odd numbers. A dining table set for guests who weren't coming.

The shift happened when I stopped decorating for an imaginary audience and started asking a simpler question: does this room support how I actually spend my time?

That question changed everything. I moved my reading chair next to the window because that's where the light is best at 4 p.m. I took the books off the styled shelf and put them where I could reach them. I stopped buying candles I was "saving" and started lighting them on a Tuesday.

Intentional living at home isn't about a certain aesthetic — though I'll always lean toward clean lines and natural materials. It's about friction. If something creates friction between you and how you want to live, it goes. If it supports that life, it stays. Simple math.

The [butter mom aesthetic](/magazine/lifestyle/butter-mom-aesthetic/) I wrote about captures a version of this — warmth and ease as design principles, not perfection. Your home should feel like it's on your side.

## Intentional Living Tips for How You Consume — Clothes, Content, All of It

Consumption is where intention gets tested most. Because we live inside systems designed to make buying feel like doing.

A new moisturizer feels like self-care. A new lamp feels like progress on the living room. A new dress feels like being ready for something. But if you're shopping to fill a gap you haven't named, no purchase will close it.

I'm not anti-buying. I love beautiful things. I believe a [well-built wardrobe](/magazine/style/capsule-wardrobe-women-over-40/) is one of the most practical investments a woman can make. But there's a difference between choosing a navy cashmere sweater because it fits your life and adding to cart at midnight because the algorithm showed you exactly what you didn't know you wanted.

The filter I use now is embarrassingly simple: **Would I go out of my way to find this?** If I wouldn't drive across town for it, I probably don't need it delivered to my door.

This applies to content too. The podcasts you listen to, the accounts you follow, the newsletters in your inbox — they're all shaping how you think, what you want, and how you feel about your own life. Curate them like you'd curate a bookshelf. Keep what earns its place.

I unfollowed about two hundred accounts last year. Not in a dramatic purge — just a quiet, ongoing edit. My feed is smaller now. It's also the only feed I've ever actually enjoyed.

## Digital Boundaries That Don't Require a Flip Phone

I know. Everyone tells you to put your phone down. But nobody tells you what to do with the discomfort that follows.

Because here's the truth: phones aren't just distraction devices. They're how we stay connected, manage logistics, run businesses, find directions. The answer isn't a cabin in the woods. It's structure.

These are the boundaries that have worked for me — not because they're radical, but because they're sustainable:

**No phone for the first and last hour of the day.** Non-negotiable. The morning is mine, and sleep is too important to scroll into.

**Notifications off for everything except calls and texts from real humans.** Every app wants your attention. Almost none of them deserve it.

**One check per platform per day.** I look at Instagram once. I check the news once. If something important happens, someone will tell me.

**A weekly phone audit.** Every Sunday I glance at my screen time. Not to shame myself — just to notice. Awareness without judgment is the most underrated tool there is.

These aren't rules. They're guardrails. And guardrails only work if you build them before you need them.

## The Relationship Between Intention and Quality

There's a reason intentional living and [quiet luxury](/magazine/style/quiet-luxury-brands/) share the same DNA. Both are about choosing fewer, better things and letting them speak without volume.

When you stop accumulating on impulse, something interesting happens: your standards go up. Not in a precious way. In a practical one. You start caring about how a sweater feels after the tenth wash, not just the first. You notice whether a piece of furniture will age well or just age. You stop tolerating the mediocre because you're no longer drowning in it.

This isn't about spending more. Some of my most intentional choices have been the least expensive — the library card I actually use, the walk I take instead of a workout class I dread, the dinner I cook slowly on a Friday instead of ordering something forgettable.

It's about the ratio of thought to action. When more of your choices are conscious, more of your life feels like yours.

## How to Handle the Guilt of Saying No

Living intentionally means saying no more often. No to events that don't interest you. No to obligations you inherited rather than chose. No to the constant, low-level pressure to be everywhere and responsive to everything.

And that will make some people uncomfortable.

Here's what I've learned: the guilt fades. The relief doesn't. Every no that's aligned with your actual priorities creates space for a yes that matters. And the people worth keeping in your life will respect the boundary — eventually, if not immediately.

I stopped attending events out of obligation about two years ago. I lost a few invitations. I didn't lose a single friendship that mattered. What I gained was weeknights. Actual, unscheduled weeknights. The kind of evening where you cook something simple, read for an hour, and go to bed without setting an alarm.

That's not a small thing. That's the whole thing.

## Intentional Living Tips You Can Start This Week

If this all feels like a lot, here's where I'd begin. Not with a full life overhaul — that never sticks. Just with awareness and one or two honest shifts.

**Audit your morning.** Track how you spend the first sixty minutes for three days straight. Don't change anything yet. Just notice.

**Choose one room.** Walk through it and ask: does every object here earn its place? Remove three things that don't. Not into the trash — just out of the room. See how it feels after a week.

**Check your subscriptions.** Email newsletters, streaming services, subscription boxes, auto-refills. Cancel anything you forgot you were paying for. If you forgot, it wasn't adding value.

**Set one digital boundary.** Just one. The phone-free morning. The notification purge. The single daily check. Pick the one that feels most urgent and commit to it for two weeks.

**Say no to one thing this week.** Something you'd normally say yes to out of guilt or habit. Notice what fills the space instead.

None of these are revolutionary. That's the point. Intentional living isn't a reinvention. It's a recalibration. Small, honest adjustments that accumulate into a life that actually feels like yours.

## The Long View

I don't have a perfectly intentional life. I still impulse-buy things I don't need. I still check my phone more than I'd like. I still say yes when I mean not really.

But the ratio has shifted. More of my days feel chosen than inherited. More of my home feels considered than accumulated. More of my time goes where I actually want it to go.

And that shift — that slow, unsexy, deeply worthwhile shift — started not with a grand gesture but with a question I keep coming back to:

*Am I choosing this, or is it just happening to me?*

Worth asking. Every day. About everything.
