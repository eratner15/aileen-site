The Claude interface blinked at me with the sort of infinite patience that only comes from not needing sleep, while I sat cross-legged on my floor at 3:17 AM on a Thursday, cold pizza grease forming a small and increasingly permanent stain on the carpet next to my laptop. My daughter’s art supplies were scattered to my left, evidence of the bedtime story I’d promised hours ago and somewhere in the background, my phone was receiving its forty-seventh notification about Docker containers, a term I still couldn’t confidently define under oath.

“*Build a gift recommendation engine*,” I told the AI, like some digital-age Aladdin rubbing a very expensive lamp.

Four hours later GiftGenius v1.0 was conjured from the ether, a team of workers who grinded for a minimal cost, one baked into a $100-a-month all-you-can-eat max plan.  

The site worked. It actually worked, sort of, which in the current economy of expectations counts as a minor miracle. Users were going to love it. 

To quote Shark Tank’s own Mr. Wonderful: **"I was going to make hundreds of dollars!” **

***[Try it yourself and find your perfect gift with GiftGenius.](https://giftgenius-mvp.netlify.app/)***

On Tuesday, September 29, 2025, at precisely 3:47 PM, OpenAI casually announced “[Buy It In ChatGPT](https://openai.com/index/buy-it-in-chatgpt/)” in an afternoon blog post delivered with all the ceremony of someone mentioning they’d reorganized their sock drawer.

Three days of work, vaporized by a bullet point in someone else’s product roadmap.

This is the fundamental contradiction of our moment. Building products has never been cheaper or easier. Yet the infrastructure to actually run them at scale requires restarting nuclear reactors that we mothballed after a little incident in 1979.

## THE CHECKBOX AT THE END OF THE UNIVERSE

I’m not alone in watching my startup product become someone else’s feature. The pattern has become so predictable it’s almost boring, except it’s happening to real people who spent real months building real things.

The body count tells the story better than any thesis statement could.

**Character.AI**: twenty million monthly users, billions raised, acquired by Google for $2.7 billion in what industry observers politely called “an acqui-hire”, Silicon Valley speak for “*we’re buying the founders back and the company can rot.*” The founders, Noam Shazeer and Daniel De Freitas, were former Google engineers who’d left in 2021 precisely because Google wouldn’t let them launch a chatbot. They raised billions building the thing Google said couldn’t work, then sold it back to Google for parts. The business model they built? Still bleeding. The talent Google wanted back? Priceless. No, actually $2.7 billion.

**Jasper.ai**: An early AI darling that quickly raised at $1.5 billion valuation, with line of sight on 100,000 paying customers, including a who’s who of major publications, only to see their plans of an AI for writing go up in flames when ChatGPT launched. The company is currently pivoting to “enterprise AI platform” while shredding VC dollars. 

**Inflection AI**: $1.3 billion raised, $4 billion valuation, sold to Microsoft for $650 million eight months later, an 84% haircut that at least returned *some* capital to investors, which is more than most AI startups will manage.

The pattern is so consistent it’s almost boring: Build something that works. Get users. Raise money. Watch a foundation model add your entire business as a checkbox feature. Pivot to “enterprise.” Die slowly while pretending you’re not dying. 

It seems so obvious in hindsight, but it doesn’t appear to be slowing down, and boy, do I want a taste.

**This pattern isn’t new, it’s just faster.** 

The half-life of innovation has compressed from years to approximately one Tuesday afternoon, somewhere between lunch and the moment you refresh your news feed and realize your entire business model just became someone else’s patch notes.

In 1876, Alexander Graham Bell filed for a telephone patent at almost the exact same moment as Elisha Gray, hours apart, possibly the same day depending on whose lawyer you believe. For decades, every telephone company paid tribute to Bell. 

In 1894, the patent expired, and suddenly there were six thousand telephone companies in America, most of them terrible, all of them competing, until AT&T consolidated them into a monopoly that lasted until 1982, when the government broke it up into Baby Bells, which gradually re-consolidated into the same monopoly we have today, except now it’s called Verizon and the customer service is somehow worse.

![](https://substack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com/public/images/6236986f-8313-40fa-b829-163a33bb654d_1024x1024.png)

That took 106 years. Character.AI went from a billion-dollar valuation to fire sale in eighteen months. We’re binge-watching the entire industrial revolution in a single season.

## ATOMIC HABITS

The four horsemen of the digital apocalypse, Microsoft, Google, Meta, and Amazon, collectively spent north of $250 billion on AI infrastructure in 2024 alone. The entire Apollo Program, adjusted for inflation, cost $280 billion over thirteen years. We’re spending moon money annually, except this time the destination is a server farm in North Dakota.

![](https://substack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com/public/images/b915487f-0434-4c58-beba-e0e2ebbd5c24_1024x1024.png)

In September 2024, Microsoft signed a 20-year power purchase agreement with Constellation Energy to restart Three Mile Island Unit 1, the nuclear reactor mothballed in 2019 because it couldn’t compete economically against natural gas. That same reactor will now deliver 835 megawatts exclusively for AI workloads, because apparently, the economics of nuclear power improve dramatically when you’re teaching computers to write better emails.

Microsoft isn’t alone in this atomic enthusiasm. Oracle is securing building permits for three small modular reactors. Amazon is committed to 1,920 megawatts of nuclear capacity. Google signed up for 500 megawatts from molten-salt reactors, which sounds like something from a Bond villain’s lair but is actually just expensive electricity with extra steps.

We are restarting nuclear reactors that couldn’t compete economically against fossil fuels to power gift recommendation engines and chatbots that help people draft emails they could have written themselves in the same amount of time.

## THE MAN IN THE LEATHER JACKET

If everyone is spending spectacular amounts of money someone has to be collecting it.

OpenAI generated $3.7 billion in revenue in 2024 and lost $5 billion. That’s $2.25 spent for every dollar earned. Anthropic burned $5.6 billion in 2024 on roughly $1 billion in revenue, a 560% burn rate that would make even WeWork blush.

NVIDIA is the friend who brings a single case of beer to the beach party and charges $40 per can because they’re literally the only person who remembered to stop at the packie. Except, instead of beer, it’s Blackwell chips, and instead of $40, it’s $30,000; everyone pays it because the alternative is not having a party at all.

The pattern from every infrastructure boom is that the people selling the shovels make money while the people digging for gold go bankrupt. The marginal cost of intelligence is approaching zero while the fixed cost of creating it approaches infinity, which means Jensen Huang doesn’t wear a leather jacket because it’s comfortable. He wears it because he can afford any jacket on Earth and chose the one that says “I’m the only 7-Eleven in a fifty-mile radius and you’re all dying of thirst.” The leather is just branding at this point; he could show up in a Snuggie and you’d still pay whatever he’s charging.

## THE ECONOMICS OF ANXIETY

Every time someone uses GiftGenius to figure out what to buy for their mother-in-law who “doesn’t need anything” and “just wants you to visit more.” The user gets a thoughtful recommendation worth maybe five dollars in saved anxiety and another hour of not having to wander Target’s candle aisle. 

Anthropic loses approximately fifty cents on compute, the Claude API call costs them 60 cents to run, but they charge me roughly 10 cents because they’re subsidizing my delusions of entrepreneurship. 

AWS charges Anthropic twenty cents for infrastructure, specifically for the Lambda functions, S3 storage, and CloudFront distribution that make the whole thing feel instantaneous, rather than like the early days of RealPlayer. 

NVIDIA’s profit, amortized across the H100 chip’s lifespan, assuming 70% utilization in a properly cooled data center: ten cents per query. And somewhere in Constellation Energy’s accounting department, a nuclear reactor marks down the 0.0034 kilowatt-hours required to prevent someone from buying another candle called “Autumn Whisper” at HomeGoods.

It’s a cascade of value destruction, where everyone loses money except the infrastructure providers. This is the Uber model perfected. Uber subsidized rides until drivers got desperate and customers got dependent. AI companies are subsidizing thought itself, except they have no labor force to squeeze, and compute costs are rising, not falling. The path to profitability requires charging people to think, which is either the future of capitalism or a cognitive toll booth, depending on your disposition toward dystopia.

## THE GHOST IN THE GDP

The confusing part is that the technology actually works. GitHub Copilot studies show developers complete tasks 55.8% faster. A BCG and Harvard study of 758 consultants found 40% higher quality results using GPT-4 for creative tasks, with 25.1% faster completion times.

Yet U.S. productivity growth in 2024 clocked in at 2.3%, better than 2023’s anemic 1.6%, sure, but still half the 3-4%+ rates from the 1990s-2000s tech boom when we were just figuring out that email was faster than fax machines and maybe we didn’t need to print everything. We’re spending more on AI infrastructure annually than we spent on the entire Apollo program, and we’re getting productivity gains that are basically a rounding error compared to “hey, what if we put spreadsheets on computers instead of doing them by hand?”

In 1987, economist Robert Solow observed, “You can see the computer age everywhere but in the productivity statistics.” Nearly forty years later, the same applies to AI, which suggests either we’re measuring wrong or the gains are going to places GDP doesn’t know how to look.

MIT research offers one explanation: After AI adoption, productivity actually declines initially due to implementation costs, data infrastructure requirements, workflow redesign, and retraining periods. The gains take four-plus years to materialize at the organizational level, which explains why 95% of enterprise AI pilots fail to reach production. The technology works. Organizations just don’t know how to use it yet, like giving a Medieval peasant an iPhone and wondering why they’re not more productive.

## I, FOR ONE, WELCOME OUR NEW LITERARY OVERLORDS

In a fit of either genius or nihilism, I responded to GiftGenius’s death by building [RatlinksGPT](https://claude.ai/public/artifacts/1c7bdc15-c0e4-4be1-85b1-5cc24572e224), a custom AI trained to write articles like the one you are reading. It took less than 30 minutes to build and cost less than lunch.

This is what expertise looks like now. Not years of craft or 10,000 hours of practice, but an afternoon of prompt engineering and a few dozen example articles. We’ve compressed the apprenticeship model into a text box and called it democratization.

## THE AFTERNOON I BECAME OBSOLETE

Here’s the thing about RatlinksGPT that keeps me up at night: it doesn’t just write like me, it writes like me on my best days. It doesn’t get tired. It doesn’t get distracted by my kids interrupting. It doesn’t spiral into existential dread at 2 AM, wondering if anyone will care about this paragraph.

When people ask me how I write Ratlinks the process is a bit fuzzy. If I run a rough draft through AI, I would go back and edit the AI’s output, but then I’d use the AI to punch up my edits, and then I’d rewritten the AI’s punch-ups in my own voice, except my own voice was now trained on the AI’s version of my voice.

I’ve become a human API wrapper 

This is what expertise looks like in 2025. 

Not deep knowledge or hard-won craft, but knowing which prompts generate output that sounds like deep knowledge and hard-won craft. I’ve spent years learning to write, and now my competitive advantage is that I can edit an AI’s attempt at my style slightly faster than someone who just discovered ChatGPT last week. It’s like being a master swordsmith in 1850, technically impressive, but the Colt revolver has entered the chat, and nobody cares how beautifully you can fold steel anymore.

The barrier to entry isn’t building the thing anymore. It’s having a taste of what the thing should be.

![](https://substack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com/public/images/b4a01ed7-a8c0-40f9-ad3a-f03645a3e781_418x411.png)

RatlinksGPT can generate infinite variations of my work. But it can’t tell you which variation is actually *interesting*. 

It can’t feel the rhythm. It can do craft. It can’t do curation. Which means the new scarce resource isn’t writing or coding or analysis. It’s judgment. We’ve automated execution and accidentally made taste the only thing that matters.

## DARK FIBER DREAMS

The AI boom is simultaneously the most democratic and most oligarchic technological revolution in history. Anyone can build a product. Almost no one can build a business.

The pattern from previous infrastructure booms is depressingly consistent. Massive overbuilding. Forty to sixty percent of companies fail. Survivors consolidate. Society eventually benefits from cheap infrastructure that wasn’t profitable for those who built it. 

The fiber optic boom left 85% of cables “dark” and bankrupted WorldCom, but that overcapacity later enabled YouTube and Netflix. The railroads transformed America, but destroyed most railroad investors. Someone always pays for the future, and it’s rarely the people who get to live in it.

The AI infrastructure being built now will probably enable applications we can’t imagine yet. The companies building it may not survive to profit from them. This is how progress works: expensive, inefficient, and ultimately inevitable, with most of the economic carnage borne by the people dumb or brave enough to go first.

## THE URANIUM STOCK OPTION

It’s 3:47 AM now. The pizza stain has achieved permanent status on the carpet, a memorial to optimism. My daughter’s art supplies have migrated to form a protective barrier around my laptop, like some crayon-based Stonehenge warding off bad product-market fit. The Docker notifications have stopped, which either means everything is working or everything has catastrophically failed, and the alerts gave up.

I open GiftGenius one last time, a captain visiting the bridge of his sinking ship. 

Seventeen queries in the last hour. Seventeen people are trying to figure out what to buy for someone they care about but don’t quite understand. The recommendations were good. The AI understood the assignment. None of those seventeen people know that in three weeks, they’ll be able to do the same thing directly in ChatGPT without visiting my site, but for tonight, GiftGenius exists and helps, and that has to be enough.

“*What should I give myself after my startup becomes someone else’s feature?”* I type, because I’m nothing if not committed to the bit.

The AI thinks for 2.3 seconds, roughly seventeen cents of contemplation at current compute costs. The answer appears, devoid of sympathy but full of an unnerving, silicon-based wisdom:

“*A sense of perspective. Or uranium stocks. Both are equally practical.”*

I laugh, and the sound is genuine. The AI is right. It’s always right now, which is either the best or worst thing about this particular moment in technological history.

My daughter will wake up in three hours. Until then, I can wonder how a site I built in a few days worked perfectly until it didn’t. About how the best ideas now have the lifespan of a long weekend, and how we’re restarting nuclear reactors to make thinking free while simultaneously making it impossible to build a business around thinking.

This concept is dead. Long live the next concept, at least until next Tuesday, when someone will announce something that makes even that obsolete.

The pizza is cold. The laptop is warm. Somewhere in a data center, a GPU is executing the next query that will make someone’s life slightly easier while burning money that may never be recovered.

This is progress. This is what it looks like. We just didn’t expect it to cost this much, or arrive this fast, or feel this much like winning and losing simultaneously.

I close the laptop. In three hours, everything starts again.
