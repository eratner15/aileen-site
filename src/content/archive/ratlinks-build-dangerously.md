In the middle of a mediocre round of golf, my friend’s phone rang for the fifth time. I know this because I was counting, which is what you do when someone keeps taking calls on a golf course, because the golf course is the last place in America where being unreachable is not just acceptable but expected. It is a monastery with beer carts. The fifth call came mid-swing. He shanked it into the trees.

He looked at his phone. He looked at the fairway. He looked at me.

“You should implement AI in my business.”

I waited for the punchline.

“I’m not joking.”

I wasn’t joking either. An hour later, I sent him a link to [cafecito-ai.com](https://cafecito-ai.com).

Not a deck. Not a doc. Not one of those Google Docs with a title like “Business Plan DRAFT v0.3 / DO NOT SHARE” that lives in someone’s Drive for eight months before everyone quietly agrees to never open it again.

A website, live, with service descriptions and an intake form and a booking link, and a domain that resolved to an actual page when you typed it into an actual browser.

![](https://substack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com/public/images/9578c0e6-fb01-4992-aa5a-59240e9c22c8_370x242.png)

He clicked it. Called me back. Became my first client.

His other business became my second client the next morning.

Then someone I’d never met filled out the [intake form](https://cafecito-ai.com/intake). And this is where the story stops being about starting a company and starts being about something I still don’t have a good word for.

The intake system didn’t just capture the lead. It had Claude read what the person wrote, assess urgency, and automatically build a pitch deck and product demo tailored to that specific prospect’s business. Then it scheduled the Zoom call. 

By the time I joined, the conversation wasn’t “here’s what Cafecito does.” It was “here’s how we supercharge what you do.” The first call skipped the overview entirely. We were already in the specifics.

That prospect became a client. That client needed a legal operations platform for his fractional practice. So I built one. [AI matter classification, corpus learning, white-label client portal, voice intake through Twilio](https://cafecito-ai.com/lawyer). A full SaaS product that exists because one real person walked through a door that a machine held open.

![](https://substack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com/public/images/f4f692cb-0c0c-4ea9-92a2-065bd9ce58e6_363x242.png)

## DEATH BY CHANGELOG

This didn’t start on the back nine. It started seven months ago on my floor next to a pizza stain that has since graduated from temporary blemish to permanent architectural feature of the carpet, which I choose to interpret as character rather than neglect.

In October, I built a gift recommendation engine at 3 AM on said floor. It worked great. Three days later, OpenAI released a feature that turned my entire product into a bullet point in someone else’s changelog. I wrote about it with what I described as self-aware humor, but it was probably just regular sadness dressed up in a funny hat.

In February, I built a tariff engineering tool overnight that does what trade consultants charge five figures for, using a government database that has been publicly accessible since approximately the second Reagan administration and that apparently nobody thought to put a search interface on until I did it at 1 AM because trade policy seemed more productive than staring at the ceiling.

The gift engine was a product. Anyone can build a product. Products get killed. I rebuilt it at [cafecito-ai.com/gift](https://cafecito-ai.com/gift), and it’s still not right. But that’s the point. It cost me an evening, not a fiscal quarter. The beauty is I can move on, and I already have.

The tariff tool was a service wearing a product’s clothes.

One dies by patch note. The other doesn’t. That distinction matters, but there was a second lesson hiding inside the wreckage of GiftGenius that took me months to recognize. Building it didn’t just teach me what survives. It taught me how to build.

Once Cafecito was on Cloudflare and I understood the actual building blocks, the APIs, secrets, workers, and routing, the problem stopped being “can I make this?” and became “what should I make next?”

I could not stop answering that question.

## LINEN LA VIDA LOCA

I need you to understand that I am about to tell you something that sounds made up but is not.

On a Royal Caribbean cruise, my kids jumped out of the pool and asked for towels. This is usually a moment you’re on autopilot. You grab the towel, you dry the kid, you go back to your drink. But I wasn’t on autopilot anymore. I hadn’t been on autopilot for weeks. I looked at the towel and thought: who supplies these? What does that logistics chain look like? How many properties in South Florida have the same problem? Is anyone solving this well? Is anyone solving this with AI?

![](https://substack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com/public/images/51c1787f-55e5-4c9a-a264-0b836ed95039_923x567.png)

I built a [towel and linen rental company](https://cafecito-ai.com/laundry/miami) from my stateroom. Not a landing page. A company. Laundry Service: Linen la vida loca. Six service verticals: HOAs and condo pools, hotels, spas, gyms, vacation rentals, restaurants. Custom quote form. A live AI phone agent that picks up the phone, captures the inquiry, and routes it to the pipeline.

The day after the cruise I was at an investor conference. During a presentation, my phone buzzed. First organic client through [cafecito-ai.com](https://cafecito-ai.com). Someone I’d never pitched, never met, who found the site and filled out the intake form on their own.

That was the conviction moment.

At the end of the presentation, I found the Cintas COO and pitched him. Not a PowerPoint about what the platform could theoretically do. A working platform that was already doing it. The demo was the product was the sales material.

And after that pitch, I became the dog that caught the bus. I had half a product and a potential client. I buckled down and built on top of what I pitched. Three market-specific sites for [Miami](https://cafecito-ai.com/laundry/miami), [Cincinnati](https://cafecito-ai.com/laundry/cincinnati), and [Dallas](https://cafecito-ai.com/laundry/dallas). An [ops dashboard](https://cafecito-ai.com/laundry/ops). Analytics. A [churn risk tool](https://cafecito-ai.com/laundry/churn-risk). [Route intelligence](https://cafecito-ai.com/laundry/route-intel). [Rep coaching](https://cafecito-ai.com/laundry/rep-coaching). Soon I had a full [Cintas demo](https://cafecito-ai.com/laundry/cintas-demo) with executive, security, ROI, and architecture presentations. An AI phone agent with sentiment analysis that doesn’t just take orders but listens for the tone that means a customer is about to churn before the customer knows it themselves.

I did not stop building on the cruise. I’m not sure I stopped building at any point during the cruise. My wife has photos of sunsets I apparently witnessed and do not remember.

[GameMaster Pro](https://cafecito-ai.com/games) got a real home. I’d already published [Peel & Eat](https://www.golfdigest.com/story/how-to-play-stableford-golf-betting-game), a golf gambling book. Now it has a [full digital scorecards app](https://cafecito-ai.com/golf) with twelve betting games that works on your phone, on the course, offline. Nassau. Skins. Wolf. Vegas. Six-Six-Six. The book became a living product. The product isn’t replacing the book. It’s making the book work while you’re standing on the 18th green trying to figure out who owes what on a five-way Nassau with carryovers while the group behind you is staring daggers at you. That’s a gap. That’s a real problem experienced by real humans in real time. 

Mah jongg is having a moment. You can look up the numbers if you want but you probably already know someone who started playing in the last two years and now owns three sets. I published a [strategy guide](https://cafecito-ai.com/games/mahj) to Mah Jongg.

## TEN THOUSAND SOMMELIERS AND NO VINTNERS

I wrote about the Sommelier Economy in [The Great Deskilling](https://ratlinks.substack.com/p/ratlinks-the-great-deskilling). The thesis: when making adequate becomes trivially easy, the premium moves to the person who knows what’s worth making. Taste becomes the moat. Your value isn’t in your ability to make wine. It’s in distinguishing between vintages that 99% of people would call “pretty good.”

The cost of building just dropped to approximately zero. Which means the market is about to be flooded with adequate. Perfectly serviceable apps and sites and tools that work fine and that nobody will pay for because there are nine hundred other versions that also work fine. The slop tsunami. We’re already in it.

You can feel it when you search for anything now. Google a recipe and you get seventeen AI-generated food blogs that all have the same paragraph about “the rich, comforting aroma that fills your kitchen” before telling you to add salt. Ask for product recommendations and you get listicles written by nobody, reviewed by nobody, useful to nobody. 

Half the internet reads like it was written by a confident intern who has never been outside. The content is adequate. The content is endless. And it is drowning the things that were actually made by people who gave a damn.

I kept asking whether we’re creating a world of brilliant curators who can’t make anything. Ten thousand sommeliers and no vintners. The last few weeks answered that question. The tools now let the sommelier become the vintner. The gap between knowing what’s worth making and actually making it has collapsed.

The Sommelier Economy isn’t just about what you choose to build. It’s about what you choose not to build. Every night I sat down, there were a hundred things I could have shipped. The filter wasn’t capability. It was taste. The mahj mom who wants to stop embarrassing herself at game night is a real gap. “AI-powered journaling for thought leaders” is not. The golfer settling a Nassau on the 18th green has a problem that exists in the physical world at a specific moment. “A dashboard for your dashboards” does not. The receptionist at a client’s office who has been answering the phone the same way for eleven years and will stage a mutiny if you replace her workflow without talking to her first is a problem that no amount of compute can solve without someone who has actually sat in that waiting room and watched what happens when you say the word “automation.”

Nobody with a trillion-dollar market cap is building for that receptionist. Those gaps are too small for companies that think in billions and too specific for platforms that think in features. They’re exactly the right size for one person with taste, tools, and an inability to stop building things at 2 AM.
