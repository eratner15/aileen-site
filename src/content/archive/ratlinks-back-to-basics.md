### The Phone That Can’t Do Anything

Last month, I watched a guy at a dinner party pull out what looked like a calculator from 2003. Gray plastic. E-ink screen. No logos.

“*Light Phone*,” he said, before anyone asked. “*No apps. No browser. No email.*”

![Light Phone III | The Light Phone](https://substack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com/public/images/85117a25-64f4-40a1-92d4-7e6879e53187_2319x2319.png)

The waitlist for the Light Phone III is 40,000 people. It costs $799. For that money, you get a device that makes calls, sends texts, and does nothing else. Less functionality than a Motorola Razr from 2005. The aesthetic of a product designed in a country with universal healthcare.

The pitch is “digital detox.” The reality is simpler: it’s the only way to stop looking at the screen.

You can’t out-discipline an algorithm. A thousand engineers designed that app to keep you scrolling. The only move is to not have the app.

A decade ago, pulling out the newest iPhone at dinner meant you were successful. Now it means you have a job that won’t leave you alone. The flex inverted. The phone that does less is now the phone that says more.

I’m not going to buy one. I lack the self-confidence to be unreachable. But I understand the appeal.
> 
> **The product isn’t the hardware. It’s the permission slip.**

### The Pyramid at the End of the World

Nekoma, North Dakota. Population 31. Thirty miles from the Canadian border, surrounded by wheat fields and wind turbines.

And a concrete pyramid.

![Stanley R. Mickelsen Safeguard Complex - Wikipedia](https://substack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com/public/images/0bcb24ac-f9c6-45ee-897b-e2a8c0292be7_4610x3599.jpeg)

No windows. No signage. Just a massive geometric slab rising from the plains like something a pharaoh left behind on the wrong continent.

This is the Stanley R. Mickelsen Safeguard Complex. America’s first and only operational anti-ballistic missile defense system. Cost: $6 billion in 1975 dollars. About $35 billion today.

The system was insane. Sprint missiles that could hit Mach 10 in seconds, accelerating so fast the air around them turned to plasma. Nuclear warheads designed to detonate in the atmosphere and knock incoming Soviet ICBMs out of the sky. The pyramid housed the radar that made it all work.

*October 1, 1975*: declared fully operational.

*October 2, 1975*: the House voted to shut it down.

One day. Six billion dollars. Congress decided the technology was already obsolete before the concrete had cured.

In 2012, a Hutterite farming colony bought the site at government auction for $530,000. They’ve let it decay. A few years ago, a guy named Mel purchased one of the underground bunkers as a survival shelter for his family. He’s not a prepper, he says. He just believes the apocalypse is coming and wants somewhere to ride it out.

I found it in the archives of the Center for Land Use Interpretation, a research group that catalogs American strangeness: nuclear waste trenches, decommissioned silos, the world’s largest parking lot.

Things we built, paid for, and immediately decided to forget.

### The Day the CIA Visited Mars

May 22, 1984. A windowless room at SRI International in Menlo Park. A man sits with his eyes closed. A researcher hands him a sealed envelope containing coordinates.

The coordinates are for Mars. The target time is one million years B.C.

This is not a conspiracy theory. This is a nine-page document on cia.gov right now, filed under the STARGATE program — the CIA’s two-decade, $20 million investigation into whether psychics could spy on the Soviets through “remote viewing.”

The subject describes pyramids. Obelisks. “Very tall, thin” beings in “strange clothes.” A dying civilization that has “accepted” its destruction.

Read the transcript. It’s formatted like an expense report. Timestamps in the margins. Researcher initials. The same bureaucratic template used for procurement audits and travel reimbursements. Somewhere in 1984, a GS-12 typed this up, filed it in a cabinet, and went to lunch.

Here’s what I can’t stop thinking about: they ran this program for twenty years.

Not because it worked. The internal assessments were mixed at best. But nobody wanted to be the guy who killed the psychic spy division. What if the Soviets had one? What if it worked and we’d abandoned it? The career risk of ending the program exceeded the career risk of continuing it.
> 
> **This is how institutions fail. Not through malice. Through the path of least resistance.**
> 
Twenty years of momentum, twenty million dollars, because shutting something down requires someone to take responsibility, and keeping it going requires only a signature.

The Martian pyramids were never the point. The point is that somewhere, right now, there’s a program just as absurd, and nobody’s going to cancel it either.

**→ [Read the transcript on CIA.gov](https://www.cia.gov/readingroom/docs/cia-rdp96-00788r001900760001-9.pdf)**

## Same Leather, Different Story

I know a woman who spends $100,000 annually at Hermès. She also owns a Birkin that cost $400.

Same leather, sourced from the same Italian tanneries that supply Hermès. Same hardware. Same stitching, down to the stitch count per inch. The only difference is one came with a receipt from a boutique in Paris and the other came from a factory in Guangzhou.

She told me this at dinner. She wasn’t embarrassed. She was proud.

This used to be underground. A subreddit called r/RepLadies, 200,000 members whispering about “Superfakes” in private Discords. Discussions more technical than engineering forums: oxidation rates of gold-plated hardware, batch codes tracked like sneaker drops, factory rankings updated monthly.

Now it’s mainstream. And it has a name: Trade War TikTok.

When Trump’s tariffs went back into effect, something unexpected happened. Chinese factory workers started posting videos. Not protests. Tutorials. “Here’s the bag we make for Dior.” “Here’s the same bag without the logo for $80.” “You’ve been paying for a story. Here’s the plot.”

DHgate, a Chinese wholesale platform most Americans had never heard of, hit #2 on the U.S. App Store. The hashtag #dupe crossed six billion views. TikTokers started comparing their $800 Louis Vuitton wallets to $100 DHgate versions on camera and couldn’t tell the difference.

Then Walmart got involved.

Last month, the retailer started listing Birkin knockoffs from third-party sellers. Seventy-eight dollars. The silhouette, the hardware, the lock — all there. TikTok named it the “Wirkin.” It sold out in every color within a week. Bethenny Frankel called it “the mob storming the castle.” The comments section coined a term for the buyers: Wirkin class.

![Walmart's Hermès Birkin Handbag](https://substack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com/public/images/86a73eb4-ff0b-4406-8762-03ecb02d8c46_259x194.jpeg)

Here’s the detail that makes it art: on the same website, in the same search results, Walmart also lists authentic Birkins from resellers. Thirty thousand dollars. Same checkout button. The algorithm shows you both and waits to see what you believe you deserve.

The tariffs were supposed to punish China. Instead, they taught American consumers where their luxury goods actually come from.

A $39,000 Birkin costs about $1,400 to produce. The European factories add the logo, the packaging, the receipt, and $37,600 to the price. Trade War TikTok made that math visible. And once you see it, you can’t unsee it.

The interesting part isn’t the fakes. It’s the clientele.

These women aren’t pretending to be rich. They are rich. They just did the math. If the copy is chemically indistinguishable from the original, what exactly is the premium buying?

The story. The receipt. The right to say “it’s real” without lying.

Hermès isn’t selling handbags. They’re selling the story that the handbag tells. And it turns out stories can be copied too — you just have to know where to look.
> 
> **The authentic Birkin has become a tax on people who haven’t found the Discord. Or, apparently, Walmart.**
>

## 30 Points of Margin

---

Everyone’s talking about tariffs. The Supreme Court might rule on them. Your uncle has opinions. Cable news won’t shut up.

Nobody’s talking about the loophole. Which is how loopholes work.

The difference between a 60% duty and a 6% duty often comes down to product design. Not trade policy. Not country of origin. The physical object itself.

A sneaker with a rubber sole: Heading 6402, 37.5% duty. Add felt to that sole? Heading 6404, 7.5% duty. Same shoe. Different spec. Thirty points of margin.

A smart speaker with built-in Wi-Fi: “data transmission apparatus,” high duty. Remove the transmitter, sell it as a $9 dongle? Main unit becomes “measuring equipment.” Duty-free.

This is called tariff engineering. Nike has a team for it. Apple has a team for it. The guy importing phone cases from Shenzhen has never heard of it. The precedents are buried in a database nobody reads.

The database is called CROSS. 219,614 rulings. All public. All free. All unreadable.

So I built something that reads it for you.

---

---

The tool pulls from two government sources in real-time: 

- 
The USITC Harmonized Tariff Schedule (every HTS code and duty rate) and 
- 
The CBP CROSS database (decades of classification rulings). 


Describe your product, and it searches 200,000+ precedents to find modifications that legally reduce your duty rate.

A friend mentioned she imports gold jewelry from Turkey. I ran her product through: 18K gold necklaces, finished, ready for retail. Current classification: 7113.19.50 at 5.5%. The tool found an alternative: import as unfinished components — chains without clasps, unset mountings — and complete final assembly domestically. New classification: 7113.19.29 at 3.5%. Bonus: She can now label it “Assembled in USA.”

Two points of margin. On a $100,000 shipment, that’s $2,000 back in her pocket. Per shipment.

The receipts are real. Every ruling ID links to the actual CBP decision. Every HTS code links to the official tariff schedule. The AI doesn’t hallucinate precedents — it cites them.

Trade consultants charge five figures for this. Enterprise software runs six. This runs on a government API that’s been public since 1989.

Trade War TikTok taught people where their handbags come from. Maybe this teaches people where their margins went.

## THE SEVEN-YEAR ITCH

---

The first edition of this newsletter went out seven years ago. January 2019. My son was eleven months old. He’ll be in high school when this thing turns fourteen.

Fourteen years is nothing. Fourteen years is everything.

Go back fourteen years from 2019, and you land in 2005. YouTube launched that year. So did Google Maps. Facebook was a year old and still required a .edu email. The iPhone didn’t exist. Neither did the App Store, nor Uber, Instagram, or the entire attention economy that now runs your life. 

In 2005, being unreachable just meant you weren’t home.

Now go forward to 2033**.**

My son will be fifteen. He’ll have spent more years with AI than without it. The interfaces we’re still figuring out — voice, agents, whatever comes next — will be as invisible to him as Google is to us. He won’t remember when you had to learn things instead of asking for them.

The jobs that feel stable right now won’t exist, or won’t look the same. Neither will the hustles. The arbitrage opportunities we’re just discovering will be automated, commoditized, and gone. New seams will open. The people who find them will look like geniuses. The people who don’t will wonder what happened.

![](https://substack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com/public/images/6fea9f3e-1887-42da-b9e8-8462c5a4ab5e_1536x1024.png)


The algorithm will be smarter. So will the counterfeits. So will the loopholes. The gap between people who see the system and people who play the game they were handed, that gap won’t close. It never does.

But here’s what I keep thinking about:

In 2005, nobody knew what was coming. Not really. The people who built the next fourteen years weren't prophets. They saw a gap or noticed some strange opportunity and followed it further than everyone else.

That’s still the whole bet. Find the thing. Follow it down. See where it goes.

Seven years from now, when this newsletter turns fourteen, I’ll either still be writing it or I won’t. My son will be finishing college or doing something that doesn’t exist yet. The world will be unrecognizable in ways I can’t predict and exactly the same in ways I can.
> 
> **The seams will still be there. **
> 
> **They always are. **
> 
> **But you have to go looking for them.**
